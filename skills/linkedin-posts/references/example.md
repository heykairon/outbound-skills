# Example: One of Luciano's Posts, From Data to Final Draft

This is a real post Luciano wrote for [Kairon's content strategy](../../content-strategy/references/example.md), and every turn it took on the way. The customer and the founders are not named here. In the published post they are, with their permission.

---

## Before writing: what his posts say

Luciano's last 100 posts were read before the first draft: 82 of them his own (reposts and other people's posts left out). Then the people who reacted to 14 of them were sorted by headline (founders, CEOs, growth, business development and GTM roles count as possible ICP).

| Post | Reactions | People read | Possible ICP | Developers and students |
|------|-----------|-------------|--------------|-------------------------|
| Friends raised millions while he renegotiated contracts | 131 | 106 | 46 (43%) | 23% |
| Co-founder at Kairon | 89 | 91 | 46 (50%) | 14% |
| A tech week in Punta del Este | 122 | 103 | 42 (40%) | 28% |
| "This guy is crazy": a founder who went to an event with no meetings booked | 166 | 104 | 34 (32%) | 20% |
| Juniors who lean too much on AI | 133 | 118 | 14 (11%) | 61% |
| "My 3 steps to 3x your pipeline" | 11 | 11 | 1 (9%) | 36% |

Posts with more than about 100 people show the first page only. "Possible ICP" is a keyword filter on headlines, so it is rough.

What it said:
- **A story about a real person, with a photo, brings the most ICP in absolute numbers.**
- **"N steps to X" posts reach almost nobody.** The tactical hook the skill suggests ("here's how: the 4 steps") failed on this audience.
- **High reactions can be the wrong audience.** The post about juniors drew 133 reactions, and 61% of them were developers.

## The turns

1. **An offer post (a campaign that failed, then the offer that worked).** Rejected: Luciano did not want to tell that story.
2. **A customer case: getting discovery calls the Mom Test way.** The real numbers came from the customer's own account in Kairon: 507 first messages in 5 months, 1 in 6 replied, about 35 calls.
3. **First draft: the message broken down part by part, as a list of quotes.** Rejected: "it doesn't work". Luciano wanted the format of his best post: a photo with the founders, and the story of what they did.
4. **Second draft: the story, lowercase and choppy.** Rejected: "I don't like the prose."
5. **Third draft: narrated, like his best post.** Luciano rewrote the turn himself: name the concept (Mom Test discovery), and say why selling cold fails (a generic message, no social proof).
6. **He asked for more depth:** the five parts of a discovery message, and how to run the call.
7. **He wrote the closing line.**
8. **Translated to English.**

The draft only became his after he rewrote parts of it. The model's job was the structure and the facts. The voice came from him.

## The final post

```
These two are crazy. [Founder A] and [Founder B] cold-messaged 507 supply chain people on LinkedIn, with no finished product to sell. They got ~35 calls:

Quick context: they're two industrial engineers building [Startup], a platform with AI agents for demand and inventory planning. They're running their first pilots.

They applied a concept that's super useful and barely used: asking for discovery calls, Mom Test style.

What most startups and companies do to get their first calls is try to sell cold and go for volume. That rarely gets a reply, because people don't connect with a generic message, and there's no social proof to back anything up.

Instead, what you have to do is ask for feedback from people who know the space. A discovery message has 5 parts:

1) What you're building, in one line. The problem you want to solve, not the feature list.

2) What stage you're at. They said they were running their first pilots and meeting people in the industry to get feedback. That tells the other person a pitch isn't coming.

3) What you don't know. Naming what you don't understand makes the ask specific, and people help when the ask is specific.

4) Why this person. Something that's only true about them: their track record in S&OP, the companies they've been at, the role they just took on. If you could send it to anyone, it doesn't work.

5) A small ask. 30 minutes, and an easy way out if they can't.

On the call, you don't do a demo. You start by repeating why you reached out, and you ask about their world: how they solve it today, the last time it happened, what they tried before. That's where you find out if the problem is real. And if it is, the sale comes on its own.

1 in 6 replied to them.

If you're trying to land your first customers and find PMF, but you're not getting enough calls, try this change and let me know how it goes.
```

## The check

| Check | Result |
|-------|--------|
| First line is a concrete, real anchor | Pass: 507 messages, ~35 calls, supply chain, cold |
| Line two says what the reader gets | Pass: the concept they applied |
| Every fact is real and from the user | Pass, once the founders confirm the number of calls. ~35 was counted from the messages where a time was agreed or an invitation sent |
| On the subject in the content strategy | Pass: go-to-market with AI, told by a founder |
| Zero em dashes, "X, not Y" or self-answered questions | Pass |
| Sounds like the user, read out loud | Pass: he rewrote the turn and the closing |
| Every line earns its place | Pass |
| Photo, video or screenshot | Pass: a photo with the founders |

Part 3 ("what you don't know") is in Kairon's discovery template, but not in the founders' messages, so the post never says they did it.
