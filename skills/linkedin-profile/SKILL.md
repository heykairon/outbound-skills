# LinkedIn Profile

Someone reads a post, gets an invitation or a message, and clicks the sender's name. In a few seconds they decide if the sender is worth following, accepting or answering. Four parts of the profile do that work: the photo, the banner, the headline and the about. Your goal is a profile where all four say the same thing to the same people.

## Before Starting

**Requires [value-prop](../value-prop/SKILL.md).** Read that skill's Before Starting first, and read the files it names. If the file that skill owns does not exist yet, do that skill first. This skill does not repeat any of it.

The headline and the banner say the value proposition to one tribe: the people in the ICP. A profile written without them says nobody's promise to nobody.

If the user has a profile, get it first: the URL, a screenshot, or the text of each part. Then judge it before you rewrite it (see "Reviewing a Profile").

**Never invent proof.** Not a customer logo, a number, an award or a result. Proof that turns out false costs the sender more than no proof. When a part needs proof the user doesn't have, say what is missing and leave it out.

---

## 1. The Photo

- **High definition and real.** Recent, good light, eyes on the camera, dressed the way the sender really dresses.
- **Status comes from the setting, not from a suit.** On a stage, at an event, in their office, with the tools of their work around them. A high-definition portrait also gives a sense of authority.
- **Nothing staged, and no borrowed setting.** A photo in a place the sender never works in is a small lie that the rest of the profile has to carry.

You can't take the photo. Say which of the photos the user has fits best, or describe the one to take.

---

## 2. The Banner

It carries four things:

1. **The value proposition**, short: the line from `.agents/value-prop.md`, cut to what fits.
2. **The tribe** it speaks to: who it is for, in their words.
3. **Social proof:** the logos of real customers, or one real result. Only what the user can back up.
4. **A call to action:** the landing page, or an invitation to talk, depending on what the user wants people to do next.

Write the banner text as those four lines, each one labelled, so the user sees that none is missing. If there is no proof yet, the banner carries three things, not a fake fourth.

---

## 3. The Headline

**The value proposition and the tribe, in one line.** "CEO at Acme" says the sender's title, and nothing about why a stranger should care.

Build it from the parts of `.agents/value-prop.md`, the ones that fit:

- **The tribe:** who it is for, in their words.
- **The dream outcome:** what they get, as a result, not as a product.
- **The time** to the first result, and **the effort** they no longer have to make.
- **Proof:** a real customer or a real number, only if the user can back it up.
- **The role and the company,** last.

Example: "Plant turnover down in 90 days, without hiring | For HR at mid-size companies in Mexico | Founder, Rotar".

**Don't open with "I help" or "Helping".** Everyone writes it, so it reads like a template. Say the result itself.

Write three options, and recommend one.

---

## 4. The About

In this order:

1. **A hook:** the value proposition, said clearly in the first lines. LinkedIn cuts the about after a few lines, so the hook must earn the click on "see more".
2. **The tribe:** who it is for.
3. **The short story** of how the sender got here: the turning point, said concretely; who they were serving then; what it taught them; what they are building now, and for whom.
4. **The mission**, in plain words.

Leave out the list of jobs, titles and dates (the experience section has them), the awards, and anything the user can't back up. Write in the first person, in the sender's voice. Keep it under a minute to read.

**Never invent the story.** If you don't know the turning point, ask for it. Don't write a plausible one.

---

## 5. One Market, All Four Parts

The whole profile points at one market.

- **One country:** aim every part at that country, in its language.
- **Several countries:** write the profile in the language of the people the user most wants to reach now. A market the user may enter some day doesn't count.

A profile in English that invites Mexican HR leaders in Spanish reads like two different people.

**Even when the user asks for another language, write the market's language first.** Say why in one line, and write the other version only if the user still wants it.

---

## Reviewing a Profile

When the user already has a profile, judge it before you rewrite it:

1. **Each part, pass or fail,** with the reason in one line: "Headline: fail. It says your title, not who you help."
2. **Does it hold together?** Do the four parts say the same promise, to the same tribe, in the same language as the messages?
3. **The one change** that matters most first. Usually the headline, because it shows next to every post, comment and invitation.

Then write the new versions of the parts that fail.
