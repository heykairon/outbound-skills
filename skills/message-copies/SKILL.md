# Message Copies

Three things decide if a stranger answers: the offer, the reason you wrote to them, and the words. In that order. Most people work on the words first. In our clients' campaigns, the same message structure got up to 20 times more interested replies with a different offer, and long, generic messages did very well when the offer was something the reader wanted for themselves. So this skill starts with the offer, and gets to the words last.

## Before Starting

**Requires [initiatives](../initiatives/SKILL.md).** Read that skill's Before Starting first, and read the files it names. If the file that skill owns does not exist yet, do that skill first. This skill does not repeat any of it.

Every message serves one initiative. The initiative names the offer and the source: the offer is the reason to say yes, and the source is the reason you wrote. Find both before you write a word. Write in the sender's voice.

If the user already sent messages, read what went out and what came back before you change anything. The replies tell you more than the template.

---

## 1. The Offer First

Rank what the message offers. From what worked best in our clients' campaigns (see [cases.md](references/cases.md)):

1. **Something the reader wants for themselves.** A job, a step in their career. These win even with long, generic messages.
2. **A free event with peers, with a date.** A small session, a dinner, a meetup, a talk, a stand at an event they go to.
3. **A request for help.** "I'm building something new, and you could help me." It works with people who can really help.
4. **A piece of work made for them.** A short review of their case, a list. See **[lead-magnet](../lead-magnet/SKILL.md)**.
5. **The product.** "Let me tell you what we do." The weakest. In our clients' data, the campaigns that offered the product got from zero to two interested replies per hundred people.

**If the offer is the product, say so before you write,** and propose a better offer from the list. Write the product message only if the user still wants it.

**Make the offer concrete.** The reader has to picture it: what it is, when, at what time, where, and with whom. "A meetup with finance leaders" is vague. "Wednesday the 21st, 4 pm, one hour, online, about what paying people abroad really costs" is an offer.

**The offer must exist,** or the user must be ready to make it when they say yes. Never claim work that was not done.

---

## 2. The Reason to Write Comes From the Source

The source of the list gives every message its first line. Say it plainly:

- **The sender's network:** "I was going through my contacts and thought of you."
- **People who reacted to a post** (the user's or a competitor's): what they reacted to.
- **A tribe:** the school, the past employer, the country they share.
- **An event:** the one the sender attends, organizes or exhibits at.
- **A signal:** what happened, and when. A new role, a hire, a post about the problem.

A real, dated fact about their situation beats any fact about their biography. "I saw you organize [the event]" works. "I saw your impressive career" is what every bot writes.

---

## 3. The Connection Note

Optional. When there is one, it carries only the reason to write, and at most the offer in one sentence: "With my partner we'll have a stand at [the event] in Lima, from the 12th to the 18th. I'd like to connect." LinkedIn caps it at 300 characters.

No pitch in the note. The note opens the door, and the message after the accept carries the rest.

---

## 4. The First Message

In this order. Not every part goes in every message:

1. **The reason you wrote,** from the source.
2. **The offer, tied to their case.** One person, one problem. Not three things you do.
3. **Proof, only if it is real.** Customers with their names, a result with its number. A named customer like them is the strongest line in the message. No proof is better than invented proof.
4. **One question, at the end.** Ask if they want the thing: "Shall I save you a seat?", "Do you want me to send it?". Not a request for a call, not even for 20 minutes: when you ask for help, ask if you can tell them what it is about. Two questions turn a message into a form.
5. **Take the pressure off:** "If it's not a good time, no problem."

Then:

- **Cut every line the message doesn't need.** If removing a word makes it unclear, stop there.
- **Send it the way the sender types it:** two or three short messages, seconds apart, rather than one block. Opener, reason and offer, question.
- **No biography of the sender.** One line on who they are is enough. In our clients' data, six lines of credentials did half as well as one line naming the event the person organizes.
- **Simple words, in the buyer's language.** If the market speaks Spanish, the message is in Spanish.

---

## 5. Follow-Ups

Silence is rarely a no. They missed it, or it came at a bad time. Send one or two follow-ups, then a goodbye:

- **One line, with a new and true reason to answer now.** "I'm closing the list for Tuesday's session. Shall I save you a seat?" A new fact also works: one customer's result, one detail of the offer.
- **Never "did you see my message?" or "just checking in".** They add nothing, and they repeat the ask.
- **The goodbye:** say it is the last message, and leave the door open. "If it's ever useful, write to me."

The words of a follow-up are usually the same for everyone. Write them once.

---

## 6. Blanks for a Model to Fill

When a model writes each message from a template, write every sentence yourself, and leave blanks only where the value changes from person to person:

- **At most two blanks:** usually the first name, and one fact about the person.
- **Each blank describes a fact to find,** not the effect you want. "{{the event they organize, from their profile}}" works. "{{something that shows I did my research}}" gives the model nothing to find, so it invents.
- **The offer, the claims and the question go in the fixed text,** never in a blank. The fixed text is the only part you are sure gets sent.

---

## Never Invent

Not a customer, a result, a mutual contact, a meeting, or work you did for them. A reader who checks and finds one false line is lost for good, and so is the sender's name. When you don't have a fact, leave the gap and ask the user.

---

## Before You Hand It Over

- Is the offer something the reader wants, and concrete enough to picture?
- Does the first line come from the source?
- One person, one problem, one question at the end?
- Is every fact real?
- Does every follow-up give a new reason?

Show the note, the first message and the follow-ups together, with the wait between them. Say which offer you chose, and why.
