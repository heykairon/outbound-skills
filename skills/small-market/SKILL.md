# Small Market

In a small market you can name every company you could ever sell to. Each person gets one first impression, and when a message fails, no new lead replaces them. So the volume cannot come from a bigger list. It comes from knowing each account, being known by it, and writing when there is a reason.

## Before Starting

**Requires [lead-search](../lead-search/SKILL.md).** Read that skill's Before Starting first, and read the files it names. If the file that skill owns does not exist yet, do that skill first. This skill does not repeat any of it.

This method is only for a market of a few hundred companies, or for the biggest accounts of a larger one. The count from lead-search decides.

Say the timeline before you start: first meetings around month three, and results you can judge around month twelve (a rule of thumb). Whoever approves the work should see it on day one, so nobody judges the method in week five.

---

## 1. Build the Whole Market, and Save It

Build the full list before you contact anyone. It is finite, so it is the asset.

- **Start from the most complete source.** In a narrow industry, an association's member list, a trade show's exhibitor list or a registry beats a LinkedIn search. Use the search for what they miss.
- **Count each buying company once.** One company can have nine LinkedIn pages and still make one decision.
- **Take out** current customers, open deals and competitors.

Save it as a file, one row per account (see "The Account List" below).

---

## 2. Map the Buyers in Each Account

A big deal has several people in it. Find 3 to 5 per account, in four roles:

| Role | What they care about |
|------|----------------------|
| **Signs the deal** | What the problem costs, in budget and risk |
| **Can block it** | What goes wrong with a bad rollout, and how to avoid it |
| **Uses it every day** | How their daily work changes |
| **Champions it inside** | One idea short enough to forward to their boss |

Your last won deals show how many people took part and which titles they had. Copy that shape. The role decides the angle of every message to that person.

---

## 3. Tier the Accounts by Fit and Signal

Score each account on two things:
- **Fit:** how much it looks like the best customers in `.agents/icp.md`, and how big the deal would be.
- **Signal:** something that happened in the last 90 days, with a date and a source. A new leader in a buying role, a job post for the role your product helps, an expansion, a funding round, an acquisition, a post about the problem. Engagement with the user counts too: count it per company, not per person. Three actions from one company in a few weeks (a reaction, a comment, a follow, a profile visit) is a strong signal.

Then three tiers:

| Tier | Who | What you do |
|------|-----|-------------|
| **A** | Strong fit and a fresh signal | Research every buyer (20 to 45 minutes per account, a rule of thumb). Write one to one. |
| **B** | Strong fit, no signal yet | Stay visible. Group them by situation. Write when a signal comes. |
| **C** | Weaker fit | Stay visible only. |

Review the tiers every 90 days. A signal moves an account up the same week.

**Tag each account with its situation:** a growth phase that broke the old process, an acquisition, a new leader. Find the situations in the won deals: what was happening inside each one when it bought. The role tells you the angle. The situation tells you which problem to lead with. Stop using a situation when it gets no replies.

---

## 4. Stay Visible to the Whole Market

From the sender's own profile, to every buyer on the list, A to C:
- Follow them, and react to their posts.
- Comment only when there is something useful to say.
- Post. Once a week, one post about the situation of one target account, from what it said in public. Tag the company, not its people. Once the person most likely to decide has accepted the invitation, send them the link: "I did some research on your company. Let me know if I got it right."

**Count how long one pass takes.** Divide the number of buyers by the warm-up touches (visits and follows) the seats can do in a week. The result is the weeks one pass over the whole market takes. If that is too long, add seats.

This is how they come to know the sender before any ask. Nobody has measured what it does to meetings. In our own campaigns on large lists, likes and visits before the invitation did not raise acceptance. So do it to be known over months, not to lift a rate.

---

## 5. Write in Waves, Chosen by Signals

Each week, take the accounts that moved to tier A. Not a fixed batch on a fixed day: writing to every tier A account at once is ordinary prospecting with a shorter list.

For each account:
1. **Research first.** The situation, the signal, and the problem you can see in public sources, each with its source and date.
2. **Look for a warm intro.** Someone the sender knows who knows a buyer beats any cold message. The ICP's sources name who those people are.
3. **Start with one person**, the role the signal speaks to: a new CFO is the signer. Bring in the other roles a few days apart, each with their own angle.
4. **Never two look-alike messages in one company in the same week.** Colleagues compare them. One sender per person. If several people send, split the company's buyers between them.
5. **Make the first ask small and dated.** A seat at a dinner or a roundtable with peers, a visit to their city, or a piece of work made for that account (see **[lead-magnet](../lead-magnet/SKILL.md)**). Never a calendar link.

**The kind of signal decides the flow:**
- **They engaged with the sender** (a reaction, a comment, a follow, an invitation): write a brief with the signal, why they fit, the problem you found in public sources, and a drafted first message. A person reviews it, edits it and sends it. If they don't reply, the same person writes the next message from the same brief.
- **Something changed at their company, but they have not engaged:** a new job, an opening for the role, a post about the problem. Send a fixed sequence, and approve each message before it goes out. The sequence: the warm-up from section 4, then an invitation without a note, then, after they accept, the dated ask from step 5.

---

## 6. Never Burn an Account

Silence is not a no. An account that does not answer goes back to tier B with a date to try again, in 3 to 6 months, and a new reason: a new signal, a new post about them, an event. After a lost deal, wait one full sales cycle. After a trial or an inbound lead that went nowhere, wait three months. Never send the same sequence to the same person twice.

**Events are the reason to write to old leads.** Before each event (the user's own, or an industry event they attend), find two groups: everyone who accepted the invitation but never replied, and the leads parked after earlier tries. Invite them to the event.

---

## 7. Measure Accounts, Not Rates

With a few hundred accounts, reply rates are noise. Track:
- **Coverage:** how much of the market is researched, and how much is reached.
- **People reached per account,** against the 3 to 5 you mapped.
- **The state of each account:** not reached, visible, talking, meeting, deal, lost, or waiting until a date.

At each review, say which accounts moved and why.

Real ones, measured by account: [three of our clients' markets](references/example.md).

## The Account List

```
| Account | Tier | Why it fits | Last signal (date, source) | Buyers (name, role, reached?) | State | Next step and date |
```

Show the first 10 rows before you fill the rest. Every fact carries its source. Never invent a signal, a buyer or a date: an empty cell is better than a wrong one.
